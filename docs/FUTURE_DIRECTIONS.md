# Future directions: what the evidence says

This note is a short research pass on evidence-based approaches to low motivation and task initiation, for the people building Depress-o-buster. For each technique it gives a description with a source, an honest reading of the evidence, the V1 features it maps onto, the seams it could inform, and what to avoid. It informs design and copy. The app is not a medical device and makes no health claims.

Bold marks V1 feature names and planned seams, as named in the product description.

## 1. Behavioural activation

Behavioural activation (BA) is a structured approach to depression: track activity and mood, then schedule gradual contact with avoided activities, acting on the plan rather than the mood. See [Martell et al. (2021), Guilford Press](https://www.guilford.com/books/Behavioral-Activation-for-Depression/Martell-Dimidjian-Herman-Dunn/9781462548385), [Jacobson et al. (2001), Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice](https://doi.org/10.1093/clipsy.8.3.255) and [Ekers et al. (2014), PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0100100).

Evidence: moderate. A meta-analysis (pooled analysis) of 26 trials supports guided, multi-session programmes; unguided app delivery is barely represented.

Already in the app:

- **Task ladders**, **Approach/Rest intervals** and **Presets**: graded task assignment (activities ranked by difficulty) with any rung allowed, plus plan-driven activity scheduling.
- **AI breakdown**, **Snooze the approach**, **Optional mood tags**, **No-guilt history** and **XP and levels**: smaller steps, plan adjustment, light monitoring, lapses as information, credit for approaching.

Could inform:

- **AI companion**: some manuals tie activities to personal values ([Lejuez et al. (2011), Behavior Modification](https://doi.org/10.1177/0145445510390929)); an optional "why this matters" line.

## 2. The five-minute rule

The "five-minute rule" is popular advice to commit to a short, bounded stint of an avoided task, because starting is the hard part. A 2025 meta-analysis confirms that interrupted tasks tend to be resumed, but finds no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks. See [Ghibellini and Meier (2025), Humanities and Social Sciences Communications](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w).

Evidence: anecdotal. No controlled trial of the rule itself was found; the resumption evidence is moderate and not about depression.

Already in the app:

- **Approach/Rest intervals**, **Presets** and **Low stimulation UI**: the rule made concrete, with one-tap start.
- **Task ladders**, **Snooze the approach** and **No-guilt history**: the commitment shrunk to one minute, continuing offered not required, the start as the success unit.

Could inform:

- **AI companion**: at rest-end, one neutral "another approach, or more rest?", never pushed.

## 3. Tiny Habits and the Fogg Behavior Model

The Fogg Behavior Model says a behaviour happens only when motivation, ability and a prompt coincide; when motivation is low, simplifying the behaviour is the lever. Tiny Habits adds an anchor in an existing routine and a brief celebration. See [Fogg (2020), Houghton Mifflin Harcourt](https://books.google.com/books?vid=ISBN9780358003328) and [Lally et al. (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674).

Evidence: weak. The model is a design heuristic, no controlled trial of Tiny Habits was found, and the habit studies are small and non-experimental.

Already in the app:

- **Approach/Rest intervals**, **Task ladders** and **AI breakdown**: the behaviour shrunk until it is doable on a bad day.
- **XP and levels** and **No-guilt history**: a partial analogue of the celebration, and silent missed days, since one missed occasion did not materially affect habit formation.

Could inform:

- **Presets**: an optional anchor sentence ("after I wake up").

## 4. Implementation intentions

An implementation intention is a plan of the form "if situation X arises, then I will do Y", made in advance for a goal the person already holds. A concrete cue linked to a small response lets action start with less deliberation. See [Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1).

Evidence: moderate. Strong for goal attainment in general samples, d = .65 (d is a standardised group difference; 0.5 is medium); samples are mostly non-clinical and the outcome is goal attainment, not mood.

Already in the app:

- **Task ladders** and **AI breakdown**: each rung is a concrete "then" response; neither supplies the cue.
- **Presets**, **Approach/Rest intervals** and **Alarms**: decisions made ahead of time, plus a recurring external cue, a loose mapping since the evidence is on self-formed cues.

Could inform:

- An optional "when/then" line on a rung or preset.

## 5. Opposite action from DBT

Opposite action is a Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) skill: name the emotion, check whether it fits the facts and helps, and if not, act opposite to the urge; for sadness, the urge is to withdraw. See [Linehan (2015), Guilford Press](https://books.google.com/books?vid=ISBN9781462516995).

Evidence: weak. The largest randomised trial is year-long, clinician-led skills training in women with borderline personality disorder ([Linehan et al. (2015), JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2205835)). Smaller randomised trials exist elsewhere, including a 24-person, 16-week waiting-list trial in treatment-resistant depression ([Harley et al. (2008), Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease](https://doi.org/10.1097/nmd.0b013e318162aa3f)), so the evidence is preliminary, not absent. Opposite action alone rests on tiny studies such as a five-client pilot ([Rizvi and Linehan (2005), Cognitive and Behavioral Practice](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1077-7229(05)80071-9)); nothing tested app use.

Already in the app:

- **Approach/Rest intervals** and **Task ladders**: a time-boxed "get active", graded like DBT's build-mastery steps; fit-the-facts stays the person's call.
- **Optional mood tags**: naming the emotion, never interpreted.

Could inform:

- **Optional mood tags**: a one-word urge note at approach start, never scored.

## 6. Temptation bundling

Temptation bundling pairs an activity a person puts off with an enjoyable one available only, or mainly, while doing it; the original field study restricted tempting audiobooks to the gym. See [Milkman, Minson and Volpp (2014), Management Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/).

Evidence: moderate. A randomised field experiment showed small gains in gym visits that decayed weekly and vanished after a holiday closure; the app's arrangement, with rest never restricted, is untested.

Already in the app:

- **Approach/Rest intervals** and **Presets**: a short "should" followed by a long "want", with nothing withheld; the mapping is structural, with no reinforcing effect claimed.
- **Snooze the approach**: keeps the pairing under the user's control.

Could inform:

- **Music integration**: a playlist tagged as approach-time, started with the approach and paused at rest.

## 7. Body doubling

Body doubling is a community-named practice, reported mostly by neurodivergent people, of starting or working on a task while someone else is present, in person or online. The main peer-reviewed evidence is one 220-person survey. See [Eagle, Baltaxe-Admony and Ringland (2024), ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing](https://doi.org/10.1145/3689648).

Evidence: weak. One descriptive survey, no controlled study of body doubling was found, and no depression sample.

Already in the app:

- None. Nothing in V1 is body doubling.

Could inform:

- **Body doubling / shared sessions**: a passive presence count ("3 others are in an approach"), shown only during an approach, with no chat or completion status; some respondents found a human double stressful.
- **AI companion**: a "just be here" option that says little.

## 8. Self-determination theory

Self-determination theory (SDT) holds that motivation and well-being depend on three needs: autonomy (own choice), competence (feeling effective at a fair challenge) and relatedness (connection). A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found expected rewards for an interesting task reduced later unprompted engagement, while positive feedback increased it. See [Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/).

Evidence: moderate. Many experiments support the model, but effects are small to moderate, the reward analysis was contested, and the tasks were lab tasks, not chores.

Already in the app:

- **Presets**, **Task ladders** and **Snooze the approach**: user-authored ratios, user-chosen challenge, and no demand without assent.
- **XP and levels**: an expected reward on completion, the category found undermining, so a caution as much as a fit.

Could inform:

- **AI companion**: prefer "that was five minutes beside the pile" over "I'm proud of you", which SDT calls controlling.

## 9. Self-compassion after setbacks

Self-compassion means meeting one's own failures with kindness rather than harsh self-criticism. In one prospective study, students who forgave themselves for procrastinating procrastinated less before the next exam. See [Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett (2010), Personality and Individual Differences](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029).

Evidence: weak. One small undergraduate sample in a single lab, and nothing tested non-judgement built into an interface.

Already in the app:

- **No-guilt history** and **XP and levels**: neutral logging, partial XP for starting, silent missed days and a never-resetting multiplier remove prompts for self-blame.
- **Snooze the approach**, **Task ladders** and **AI breakdown**: "not ready yet" and "too hard" are answered with time or a smaller step, not criticism.

Could inform:

- Post-abandon flow: straight into rest with a one-line log ("Ended at 1:40. Logged."). A hypothesis.

## 10. Gamification and streaks

Gamification adds points, levels, streaks and leaderboards to non-game apps to encourage repeated use. A field study found that a log showing a broken streak reduces later engagement. See [Silverman and Barasch (2023), Journal of Consumer Research](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/49/6/1095/6623414).

Evidence: weak. The broken-streak finding rests on one paper, and nothing was tested with people low in energy.

Already in the app:

- **XP and levels** and **No-guilt history**: a multiplier that never shows a broken state, and silent missed days; 69% continued after a broken streak versus 93% with an intact one, and repair only softened that (85%).

Could inform:

- **Sync and backup**: a restore that zeroes XP would show exactly the loss that reduces engagement, so preserve the multiplier.

## 11. Engagement, retention and the wellness boundary

Real-world use of consumer wellbeing apps is brief: across 93 popular apps, median 30-day retention was 3.3%. US guidance treats software limited to relaxation or stress claims, naming no condition, as general wellness, not a medical device. See [Baumel et al. (2019), Journal of Medical Internet Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720) and [FDA (2026), General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices).

Evidence: moderate. The retention figures are well supported but descriptive, and the guidance describes policy, not outcomes.

Already in the app:

- **No-guilt history** and **XP and levels**: lapsing is typical, not a failure, and the never-resetting multiplier suits intermittent use.
- **Optional mood tags** and **Task ladders**: one optional word with no scores, and everyday activity with no reference to a condition.

Could inform:

- Store listing and onboarding: one line that the app is a timer and activity tool, not a medical device; the name references depression and deserves review.

## 12. Work-rest intervals and the Pomodoro technique

The Pomodoro Technique divides work into fixed intervals, classically 25 minutes, each followed by a short break; a meta-analysis of breaks under ten minutes finds small self-reported gains and no performance effect. See [Albulescu et al. (2022), PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460), [Biwer et al. (2023), British Journal of Educational Psychology](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel) and [Smits, Wenzel and de Bruin (2025), Behavioral Sciences](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/).

Evidence: weak. Micro-breaks have support for small self-report gains only; no study tested a short-work, long-rest ratio.

Already in the app:

- **Approach/Rest intervals**, **Alarms** and **Snooze the approach**: the two-timer loop, its ring, and an extension; the 20-30 minute rest sits outside the tested dose.

Could inform:

- Self-ended rest with an "I'm ready" button, which two of three trials support.

Avoid:

- Shipping 25/5 as the default or calling it the "correct" ratio: the one trial using exactly 25/5 (Smits et al.) found no overall difference from self-chosen timing, and the trial favouring fixed breaks (Biwer et al.) used 24/6 and 12/3, so nothing singles out 25/5.

## 13. Graded exposure and approach behaviour

Graded exposure breaks an avoided situation into a ladder of steps and keeps the person in contact with each, approaching rather than avoiding; current theory holds that strict order matters less than once assumed. See [Craske et al. (2014), Behaviour Research and Therapy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/).

Evidence: moderate for clinician-delivered exposure in anxiety; the mechanism (fear extinction) is not the app's target, and no study tests a self-guided proximity timer.

Already in the app:

- **Task ladders** and **AI breakdown**: any rung allowed, since Craske et al. do not proceed linearly, plus intermediate steps on demand.
- **Approach/Rest intervals** and **Snooze the approach**: a bounded stay near an avoided cue that ends on time, not on feeling.

Could inform:

- **AI companion**, **Music integration** and **Body doubling / shared sessions**: comforts that can blunt exposure learning, so keep each optional and never tied to credit.

## 14. Saying intentions out loud

Saying an intention out loud, privately, is one of the app's lowest rungs. Whether being heard by others helps is unsettled: four small experiments found intentions noticed by others were acted on less, while a 141-trial meta-analysis found public goals more effective. See [Gollwitzer et al. (2009), Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2009.02336.x/) and [Epton, Currie and Armitage (2017), Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/unique-effects-of-setting-goals-on-behavior-change-systematic-rev/).

Evidence: weak. The backfire rests on 168 students in one lab; nothing tests private spoken self-statements in an app.

Already in the app:

- **Task ladders**: the rung "say out loud 'I'd like to do laundry'" is a private, self-written statement, not a public commitment.
- **No-guilt history**: records what happened, not what was announced.

Could inform:

- **Task ladders**: an optional rung family from "I'd like to" to "I will", no strength treated as better.

## Things this app deliberately avoids

Merged and de-duplicated; numbers are the source sections.

- Streak loss, resets, decay to zero, freezes or repair tokens: a broken-streak display lowers later engagement (3, 9, 10, 11).
- Rest as something to earn: the cited trials did not withhold rest (1, 5, 6, 8, 12).
- Guilt, "should", consistency-pressure or cheerleading copy: pressure makes action feel externally driven (1, 8, 9, 14).
- Locked rungs, mandatory order, auto-escalation, mandatory plans or "keep going" prompts: ladders are orderings, not gates (1, 2, 3, 4, 13).
- Mood-contingent prompting or interpreting mood tags: activities follow a plan, not a mood (1, 5, 11).
- Unsolicited notifications, "we miss you" campaigns, or engagement as the goal: nagging is what "AI never nags" rules out (8, 10, 11).
- Public commitment, accountability partners, leaderboards, or public rooms by default: exposing lapses invites evaluation (7, 8, 14).
- Treatment, therapy or clinical language, branding features as BA, DBT or exposure, "research-backed" copy, or "66 days" countdowns: every strong result is clinician-delivered, and such claims leave the wellness policy (all).
- Markers on unfinished tasks, gated unlockables or randomised rewards: no recall advantage was found, and locked content decayed after a break (2, 6, 10).
- Advertising the XP payout as the reason to start: expected rewards undermined later engagement (8).
- Shipping 25/5 as the default or treating five minutes as a minimum: nothing singles out either number (2, 12).
- Distress ratings, fear language, or mandatory companion, music or body doubling: comforts are comforts only if optional (7, 13).

## What the ladder is, in these terms

In behavioural activation terms, a task ladder is graded task assignment: one avoided activity split into steps ranked by difficulty, aimed at contact rather than completion. The approach interval is the scheduling half, a slot that starts because the plan says so. In Tiny Habits terms, the lowest rungs are the behaviour shrunk to a bad-day size, and the rest-done sound is an anchor for the next one. The exposure literature adds that strict order matters less than once assumed, so any rung can be chosen. None of this has been tested as a self-guided timer, so the ladder counts small approaches; it is not a method with known effects.
